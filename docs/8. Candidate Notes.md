## 1. Elevator Pitch

**The Problem:** The Ford GoBike System Data covers **183,412 bike-share trips** taken during **February 2019** in the San Francisco Bay Area. Each record holds trip times and duration, stations, bike ID, and rider attributes: user type (Subscriber vs. Customer), gender, and birth year. The raw durations span roughly three orders of magnitude (61 seconds to 85,444 seconds), many rows lack birth years, and two very different rider populations are blended in every raw total. The task was a two-part project: an exploratory notebook that follows the Question–Visualization– Observation framework, and a second, presentation-ready notebook whose insights must match the first.

**The Solution:** Two executed Jupyter notebooks implement a reproducible pandas/NumPy/Matplotlib/Seaborn pipeline. A single cleaning routine (`clean_bike_data` in Part I, reused as `load_and_clean` in Part II) drops rows with null or zero durations, converts duration seconds to minutes, parses `start_time` into `hour_of_day` and an ordered `day_of_week`, derives `member_age` from `member_birth_year` and keeps plausible ages (16–80), and casts descriptive columns to categorical dtype. Reusable plotting helpers (`plot_hist`, `plot_count`, `add_labels`, `pivot_hour_by_day`) keep formatting consistent across eight exploratory figures and four presentation figures.

**The Result:** Cleaning retains **174,955 trips × 20 columns** (8,457 rows removed, all by the age filter). Trips are **strongly right-skewed** (median **8.52 min**, mean 11.74 min; 96.55% of trips finish within 30 minutes), and a log-scaled axis reveals a near-log-normal peak around 9–11 minutes. **Customers average roughly 2× the trip duration of Subscribers** (means 21.84 vs. 10.68 min; medians 13.0 vs. 8.17 min), and exceed an hour at ~10× the Subscriber rate (4.22% vs. 0.43%). Weekday trips spike at **8 AM and 5–6 PM**; weekends shift to a broad midday band (59.1% of weekend trips between 10 AM and 4 PM). Age peaks in the **late 20s–early 30s** (median 32) but shows essentially no relationship with duration (r = 0.029 on the log scale), and gender drives **volume** (Male 74.6% of trips) rather than duration. All findings are bounded by a one-month sample; no causal claims are made.

### Key Design Decisions

| Decision | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Convert durations to minutes** | Seconds are the raw unit but minutes read naturally for 5–20 minute trips; the variable of interest is renamed `duration_min` once and used everywhere. |
| **Log-scale duration plots** | Durations span 1.02 to 1,409 minutes (~3 orders of magnitude). A linear axis collapses 96.55% of trips into a few bins; log-spaced bins plus an `xscale('log')` axis resolve the near-log-normal body. |
| **Derive `member_age` with an explicit 16–80 bound** | Birth years range 1878–2001; ages beyond plausible riding range are data errors. The bound is documented with exact drop counts (8,457 rows) so its effect on later claims is visible. |
| **Order `day_of_week` Monday→Sunday as a categorical** | Heatmaps and facets must present weekdays in reading order. A `CategoricalDtype` guarantees that instead of relying on plot-time sorting. |
| **Keep `hour_of_day` as 0–23 integers** | The 24-column heatmap grid and facet histograms share one bin definition (`np.arange(-0.5, 25.5, 1)`) so every time plot is directly comparable. |
| **Report counts and shares together** | Frequency bars carry annotated counts *and* percentages; group statistics are printed with `count` columns so a 2× duration ratio is read against real denominators (16,622 vs. 158,333). |
| **Facet before concluding about time patterns** | The combined heatmap mixes two populations. Faceting `hour_of_day` by user type showed that the Customer pattern is a broad midday hump (46% of its trips between 10 AM–4 PM), which the combined view alone would have hidden. |
| **Add a claim-verification cell to each notebook** | Every number quoted in the markdown is recomputed and printed in one place, so narrative and executed outputs cannot drift apart. |
| **Reuse one cleaning function across both notebooks** | Part II's `load_and_clean()` replicates Part I's `clean_bike_data()`, so the presented data is identical to the explored data - same filters, same derived columns. |

## 3. Analytical Principles in Practice

**Check the data before interpreting it.** The notebooks open with shape, dtypes, `.info()`, `.describe()`, and `.head()` on the raw table. Those outputs establish the baselines quoted throughout: 183,412 × 16 source, 8,265 missing birth years, and the full raw duration range.

**Make every transformation explicit and count it.** Cleaning is documented per step, and the drop counts are printed: 0 rows by the duration filter, 8,457 by the age filter. No step silently reshapes the sample a claim depends on.

**Respect the level of the claim.** Distribution statements (right skew, peak location) come from histograms plus quartile summaries; group statements (2× duration) come from grouped medians and means; count statements (74.6% Male) come from normalized value counts. Nothing is asserted about one level from evidence at another.

**Pair rates with denominators.** The Customer "outliers" comparison could mislead in raw counts (702 vs. 688 trips over an hour). Phrase it as a **rate** - 4.22% of Customer trips vs. 0.43% of Subscriber trips - which is both accurate and 10× more meaningful.

**Look for interactions instead of trusting aggregate shapes.** Duration barely varies with age; the age×duration cloud's shape repeats across all three gender levels (per-gender correlations −0.002 to 0.046); the weekday commute ridges disappear inside the Subscriber facet, not the Customers'. Layered views, not single-variable summaries, earned these conclusions.

**Treat suggested findings as hypotheses, not facts.** Conclusions suggested before the data is examined (e.g., "older members take slightly shorter trips") are verified or rewritten. The dataset's actual r of 0.029 buried that hypothesis, and both notebooks now say what the data shows.

**Keep association and causation separate.** Duration-by-user-type differences describe rider populations, not contract effects; the commute spikes describe February 2019, not all months. No causal language appears in the notebooks.

## 4. AI Collaboration Playbook

The project's deliverables are two notebooks plus HTML exports whose claims must match executed cells. The executed notebook outputs were treated as the single source of truth for every number in prose - both when writing the notebooks and again in a later phrasing audit.

The workflow was:

1. Probe the raw CSV once, up front, and record the exact post-cleaning counts, quantiles, group statistics, and time-pattern counts before writing any markdown.
2. Write every observation from those printed values rather than from assumptions, reusing template rubric text only where the data agreed.
3. After completion, run a claim-by-claim audit: extract every markdown sentence carrying a number, and recompute its supporting statistic independently.
4. Flag mismatches (a "8–28 min" IQR that is actually 8.3–20.5; a "~1.5× weekend reduction" that is actually 2.02×; an absolute "no commute signature" claim with a visible 2,024-trip bump) and correct the markdown from computed values.
5. Add a **claim-verification cell** to each notebook that prints every quoted statistic, making the narrative traceable inside the deliverable itself.

The key collaboration principle is **evidence-first editing**: executed outputs ground the narrative, prompt-suggested conclusions are checked against data before being transcribed, and any claim found in prose but not in a printed output is either supported by a new computed cell or removed. Consistency between the two notebooks is enforced by constructing Part II's summary directly from Part I's verified results.

## 5. Evaluation & Verification Philosophy

**Executed outputs are the evidence.** Every headline number came from executed cells: source dimensions, cleaning toll, quartiles, grouped medians/means, normalized counts, hourly pivots, and correlations. The two claim-verification cells keep that evidence inside the notebooks.

**Quote the computed value, not a comfortable round number.** The median is 8.52 minutes - written as "≈ 8.5 min" only after the exact value existed; the Customer/Subscriber mean ratio is 2.04, reported honestly as "roughly 2×".

**Verify the shape claims, not just the point estimates.** "Right-skewed" is supported by mean 11.74 vs. median 8.52; "peak in the late 20s–early 30s" by the modal age 31 with the top six ages spanning 26–31; "peaking around 9–11 minutes" by the modal log-bin 9.42–11.34. Distribution language is anchored to computed summaries, not to eyeballing the plot.

**Recompute trend claims numerically.** The "no strong age effect" statement is backed by r = 0.029 (age vs. log-duration) and per-age-band profiles with 90th percentiles between 18.7 and 22.5 minutes - not by the scatter's look alone.

**Keep retentions visible.** The 16–80 age filter removes 8,457 rows (4.6%). It matters for age-related claims specifically; duration and time-of-day findings should be quoted knowing the filter ran first, and the exact counts make that auditable.

**Rendering is a separate quality gate.** Execution correctness (0 error outputs across 23 code cells) was verified programmatically; the 12 rendered figures were then inspected visually in a contact sheet before submission. Charts, medians, and annotated labels were checked against the same computed values as the prose.

## 6. Challenges & Resolutions

### Challenge 1 - A three-orders-of-magnitude duration distribution

**What happened:** Trip duration runs from 1.02 to 1,409 minutes. On a linear axis almost all 174,955 trips collapse into the first few bins, and the "distribution" is invisible.

**Resolution:** Use log-spaced bin edges (`np.logspace` between the observed min and max, 40 bins) plus `xscale('log')`. The result is a near-log-normal body peaking at the 9.42–11.34-minute bin, which is also correctly stated as "peaking around 9–11 minutes" rather than a rougher guess. The tail (min 1.02 → max 1,409 min, ≈ 3.1 orders of magnitude) is reported as the reason for the transformation.

### Challenge 2 - Suggested conclusions vs. computed conclusions

**What happened:** The project's planning notes suggested observations such as "median around 9–10 minutes" and "older members take slightly shorter trips". The data did not agree: the median is 8.52 minutes, and the age-vs-log-duration correlation is 0.029 with the oldest band (56+) showing the *longest* 90th-percentile tail (22.5 min vs. 19.1 for ages 26–35).

**Resolution:** Both notebooks state the computed findings. Part II's executive summary was built from Part I's verified results so the two documents cannot disagree: insight 4 now reads that age distribution peaks in the late 20s–early 30s with no meaningful effect on trip length. Suggested conclusions were treated as hypotheses, and only the verified ones survived.

### Challenge 3 - Overplotting 175,000 points

**What happened:** At 174,955 points, a plain scatter of age vs. duration is solid ink; adding color for three gender levels makes it worse.

**Resolution:** Two treatments, chosen per plot: the plain age scatter uses very low alpha (0.05), tiny markers, and `rasterized=True`; the gender-coded scatter uses `s=8`, alpha 0.3, and hue ordering. The dense horizontal band (5–20 minutes) remains readable in both, which is what makes the honest conclusion possible: the band's shape repeats across genders.

### Challenge 4 - Two usage patterns hidden in one heatmap

**What happened:** The day-of-week × hour heatmap shows sharp weekday commute ridges and a weekend midday band - but 90.5% of trips are Subscribers, so the combined view mostly shows them, and the Customer contribution could be assumed or guessed.

**Resolution:** Facet `hour_of_day` by user type with a shared y-axis. This quantifies the split: Subscribers' top hours are 17 (18,886) and 8 (18,699); Customers' busiest hour is also 17, but with only 2,024 trips, and 46.0% of Customer trips land in the 10 AM–4 PM window. The Part I narrative and Part II's Visualization 4 both present this as the interaction finding - one apparent time pattern, two different rider rhythms.

### Challenge 5 - Absolute claims that the data did not support

**What happened:** During the phrasing audit, several written observations failed against computed values: the Customer box "whole interquartile range (roughly 8–28 min)" is actually 8.3–20.5 minutes; the weekend "1.5× fewer trips" is actually 2.02× (14,475 vs. 29,201 average trips per day); "Customers have no commute signature at all" - yet their 5 PM bar (2,024 trips) is real and visible; and the age-variance story (younger riders' longer trips) had no support in any age band's spread.

**Resolution:** Every mismatched sentence was rewritten from the computed value, in both notebooks, with the number now printed in the notebook's verification cell. The long-tail/outlier comparison was reframed from raw counts (702 vs. 688 - nearly equal) to rates (4.22% vs. 0.43%) to avoid the count/rate trap.

### Challenge 6 - Keeping every narrative number traceable

**What happened:** Numbers quoted in markdown (users shares, 60+ rider share, trough timing) originally circulated only inside plot annotations and images, or came from an external probe script. Nothing inside a notebook could confirm them.

**Resolution:** Each notebook gained one claim-verification code cell that recomputes and prints every quoted statistic - the duration distribution, grouped duration summaries and >60-minute rates, age-profile and correlation values, weekday/weekend counts, per-user-type hourly shares, and gender counts with medians. Claims without supporting output were either given one or removed (e.g., the workspace-size sentence, which is not a data claim).

## 11. Retrospective

### What I'd Do Differently

1. **Write observations only from printed outputs, from the first draft.** Several early sentences mixed computed numbers with approximate phrasing ("6–12 minutes", "8–28 min" IQR). Computing the modal bin and grouped quartiles *before* writing the sentence would have made the audit shorter.

2. **Include the claim-verification cell from day one.** It was added during the audit; had it existed initially, the notebook-reviewer loop would have been shorter and the "unverifiable claim" category couldn't have emerged.

3. **Run a sensitivity check on the 16–80 age filter.** The filter removes 8,457 rows (4.6%), which the notebooks disclose, but a side-by-side of key duration statistics with and without the filter would show whether the age findings depend on the bound.

4. **Quantify the age relationship beyond a single r.** Per-age-band profiles exist in the audit, but a small grouped table (median/90th percentile per band) printed next to the scatter would let readers see the flat spread pattern directly.

5. **Use more than one month for the time-pattern claims.** February 2019 weekday/weekend patterns are crisp, but they are one snapshot; a multi-month import would support (or correct) the commute-vs-leisure generalization before it informs capacity planning.

### What Worked Well

1. **Probing the data before writing any markdown.** One up-front script captured the cleaned shape, quartiles, group statistics, and hourly counts, so observations, templates, conclusions, and Part II's summary were all written from the same verified numbers.

2. **The Question → Visualization → Observation framework.** Every exploratory figure sits between an explicit question and a recorded observation, which made the final audit near-mechanical: each claim in a QVO cell mapped to one output.

3. **Log-spaced bins for durations.** The single most consequential plotting decision: it turned an unusable skew into the project's clearest chart and made the median-line annotation in Part II meaningful.

### The AI Partnership

> "The value of AI in this project was not a polished deliverable by itself - it was keeping the narrative chained to the executed analysis. The probe set the numbers; the notebooks quote them; the audit recomputes them and corrects prose when they disagree, including conclusions that had been suggested but were not true of this dataset. The printed claim-verification cells inside the notebooks are the mechanism that keeps the two-sided agreement from being re-falsified later. The human role was to insist that suggested findings be checked, that mismatched claims be rewritten from computed values, and that association language stay descriptive. The AI accelerated the probing, drafting, and diffing; it did not excuse the verification."

The most useful lesson is that **consistency across a two-part deliverable is structurally enforced, not intended**: one cleaning function, one shared set of printed statistics, and one verification cell per document are what make Part II's executive summary incapable of silently diverging from Part I's conclusions.
